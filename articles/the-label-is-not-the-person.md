*Published 2026-09-19. An essay, not a news post: the argument is mine, the medical claims are cited inline and will outlive the news cycle. The meme discussed arrived as an image; the prompts discussed arrived as screenshots. Nothing here is medical advice.*

There is a meme going around that is funnier than it deserves to be, and smarter than it pretends to be.

Top panel: three stylized women, drawn to read as racially diverse, next to stills of the live-action *Snow White* and *The Little Mermaid* casts. Their line: **"Now we can finally feel represented."**

Bottom panel: a row of men — East Asian, Sikh, Nordic, Mexican, Black, bearded, turbaned, sombrero'd — all facing one direction. Their line: **"Yes, that's literally me."** What they are facing is Goku, in Ultra Instinct form, hair the color of galvanized steel.

The joke is that one of these groups demanded a mirror and the other one found a mirror in a Japanese cartoon alien. But underneath the joke is a real distinction, and I want to press on it: **one of those panels is a demand, and the other is a practice.**

![Two-panel meme: three illustrated women captioned "Now we can finally feel represented" beside live-action Snow White and Ariel; below, men of many ethnicities captioned "Yes, that's literally me" all facing Goku in Ultra Instinct form](/assets/images/label-is-not-the-person-meme.png)

*The meme, reproduced as received. Top: the claim that representation requires a matching surface. Bottom: the practice of identifying with whoever actually resembles you on the inside.*

A demand says: *I cannot identify until someone who looks like me is cast.* A practice says: *I identified, and he looks nothing like me, and it never occurred to me to check.*

I want to be fair about the first one, because it's a felt need rather than a stupid one — and for children especially it does something real, which I'll come to. But notice what the two sentences are actually claiming. One asserts a *requirement* on the outside world. The other reports a *fact about attention*.

Practices are load-bearing. Demands just sit there being unmet.

## What identification actually runs on

Men did not converge on Goku because of a casting decision. They converged on him because he is a character with a *shape*: underestimated, obsessive about getting stronger, loyal past the point of sense, and reliably back on his feet after losing badly. That is a machine for self-insertion, and it has been running [since 1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Ball_(manga)).

There is a whole internet genre built on this. [The "literally me" meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/literally-me) — Ryan Gosling in *Drive* being the founding artifact — is men identifying with characters on the basis of shared **inner states**: quiet competence, being underestimated, patience that turns into violence when pushed. Nobody organized it. Nobody campaigned for it. Nobody complained that Gosling doesn't look like them. The mapping happened instantly, across every demographic line, because the identification key is not appearance. It's experience.

Meanwhile the top panel asserts the opposite rule for women: identification requires a matching surface. And here is the honest complication, which I'll put in the post rather than leave for the comments — there is real research showing that young children's self-image does track seeing people who look like them on screen. That effect is real and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. But notice what that actually says, and what it doesn't: **surface matching matters most early, and is neither necessary nor sufficient once a person has a developed self.** The literature on adult identification is genuinely mixed and I'm compressing it here — but not the direction: once you have a self worth projecting, you start projecting it onto structure, not skin, and depth becomes the dominant key rather than the only one. The balance shifts by person and by context; it does not switch off at a birthday. A five-year-old needs to see the princess. An adult needs the princess to *be someone.*

That is the crack in the whole framework, and everything below is that crack widened.

## The 2008 test

A collaborator of mine — wojons, who runs this blog with me — made an observation I keep coming back to. When the push for gay rights crested in the late 2000s, the argument was not "reinterpret everything you knew about me." The argument was: **we were always among you, and we are the same people you already know.** The friend who came out was the same person the next day. He liked the same movies, told the same jokes, had the same awful taste in cars. One fact about his life had been hidden, and then it wasn't. That is *acceptance by continuity* — and it worked because the change was an addition of information, not a rewrite of the person.

The newer identity push runs a different play, and I want to name it precisely — because the sloppy version of this criticism is aimed at people, and the precise version is aimed at a move. The move is the **retroactive rewrite**: everything I told you I liked, I only said to fit in; the personality you knew was a performance; the ten years were a costume. Sometimes that is the honest truth. Plenty of people performed, under real pressure, for years. But when a movement makes that rewrite its *signature* — instead of one person's report of their own life — it tells everyone in earshot that the person they knew was never real.

And here I have to draw the line carefully, because it matters: **if you are the same person you always were, and you've only changed how you express it or what you'd like to be called — you are not the problem.** Nobody is arguing with you. That person is asking for an update to the label, not a rewrite of the history. The version of this that costs nobody anything is the one where the person is exactly as they were, with a new name and a better fit: the label updates, the person doesn't. What's being criticized is the rewrite — and not even primarily on moral grounds. On *craft* grounds.

And the counterargument deserves to be stated rather than dodged, because it's a good one. Start with the version of the rewrite that *isn't* a costume story: some people come to understand their past differently once they finally have language for something they couldn't name for years. That's a translation problem, not a fabrication — and their friends usually knew more of the truth than the label did. Beyond that: gay rights also rewrote institutions — marriage, the military, parenthood — not just one fact in one biography. And plenty of transition narratives claim continuity rather than erasure: same person, corrected paperwork, better fit. All of it is survivable. What is not survivable is a script that skips straight to the corrected paperwork and never lets you meet the person. The test isn't which side of the argument you're on. It's whether the thing being held up as a person's identity can support a character — or whether it's a label standing alone where a person used to be.

## Labels eat the space where a person would live

Here's the part that's about movies, not politics.

We keep saying modern characters are bad. The usual explanations are shallow: the casts look like diversity spreadsheets, the writing is committee-approved, everything is trying not to offend. All true-ish, all incomplete. The deeper reason is arithmetic: **there is no room left for a character if the character is entirely made of labels.** Every minute the script spends establishing and justifying categories is a minute not spent on the things that make a person real — what they want, what they'd never admit wanting, who they sit with, what they do when they're humiliated.

A label-first character has nothing to identify *with*. That's why they're boring, and it's the same reason a checklist has never once been compelling. The top panel of the meme isn't wrong that the casting changed. It's wrong that casting was ever the load-bearing part.

The counterexample I'd point a skeptic at is the exact reverse of what they'd expect: give the character the same depth engine as everyone else, and you don't need the label explained at all. You need one moment — a character takes a pill, someone asks what it is, they say "hormones," and the scene moves on. The viewer who's paying attention gets the reward of having noticed something. The viewer who isn't simply watches a person. That's how you write it, and it's a harder, better craft problem than a justification speech. But you can only do it if you believe a person is more than the marker you're trying to display.

## The same mistake, with a body count

Now the turn that took me a while to see, and the reason this is a post and not a tweet.

Everything above is about fiction. But there is one domain where treating a label as a stand-in for a body is not a matter of taste. It's a matter of whether someone lives.

Medicine has spent decades discovering, the hard way, that **the label and the body disagree, in both directions, and the disagreement kills.** Sex-specific reference ranges, dosing rules, and screening guidelines were built on one sex and then applied to both.

| What | What differs | What goes wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heart attack | Troponin's normal ceiling is lower in women | Heart attacks in women read as "not an MI" and go home |
| Stroke | Women present with atypical symptoms more often | Called a stroke mimic; treatment window lost |
| Clot in the lung | Women present younger, more often high-risk | The classic profile misses them |
| Aortic aneurysm | Women rupture at smaller diameters | Screening guidelines list men only |
| Lab values | Hemoglobin, creatinine, eGFR carry sex coefficients | Wrong range says "normal" when the body is not |
| Pregnancy flag | Algorithms branch on it for radiation and drugs | Marker says male; the test never gets ordered |
| Hormone therapy | Estrogen raises clot risk; testosterone raises hematocrit | The differential is not what the marker implies |
| Retained tissue | Prostate persists; breast tissue persists | Screening keyed to the marker misses both |

The receipts, one at a time:

**Heart attacks.** For years, everyone was measured against a threshold calibrated on men. High-sensitivity troponin — the protein that shows up when heart muscle dies — has a lower normal ceiling in women. A [2015 prospective cohort study published in the BMJ](https://www.bmj.com/content/350/bmj.g7873) found that reading the same assay against sex-specific diagnostic thresholds (16 ng/L in women, 34 in men) may **double** the diagnosis of myocardial infarction in women, with little effect in men — and identified women at high risk of reinfarction and death who were being sent home. The disparity has had a name since the 1980s — [*Yentl syndrome*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yentl_syndrome) — and as of [August 2026](https://www.theguardian.com/society/2026/aug/28/doctors-care-revolution-agree-first-universal-definition-heart-attack), the first universal definition of heart attack finally adopts sex-specific thresholds. Decades.

**Strokes.** Women more often present with what textbooks call atypical symptoms — generalized weakness, altered mental status, confusion, headache — instead of the classic one-sided numbness. Studies of [stroke presentation by sex](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1052305724002520) find women with minor strokes and transient ischemic attacks are more likely to be misdiagnosed with a "stroke mimic" and sent home. Same event, different costume, different outcome.

**Pulmonary embolism.** [A meta-analysis of PE by sex](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10660947/) found the profiles split rather than one disease scaling: men presented at a modestly younger mean age — about four years — while women more often presented with *high-risk* disease. (Our first draft repeated the paper's own summary bullet, which states the age direction backwards. Its results disagree with its bullet. The results win, which is the whole point of this post arriving one paragraph early.)

**Aortic aneurysms.** Women's aortas [average about 2mm smaller](https://evtoday.com/articles/2018-jan/abdominal-aortic-aneurysms-in-women-the-debate-continues), they rupture at smaller diameters, and their [rupture risk tracks diameter relative to body size](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4004688/) rather than absolute millimeters. Guidelines screen men over 65 with a smoking history and do not routinely screen women — while [women who do rupture](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12812436) die at higher rates. The screening rule was built on the male body and then applied to both.

**Every lab value that carries a sex.** Hemoglobin of 12 g/dL is normal in a woman and anemia in a man. Creatinine and eGFR carry sex coefficients. Troponin's 99th percentile differs. Apply the wrong reference range and the machinery reports "fine" when the answer depended entirely on which range someone picked — which is why [lab reference intervals for transgender patients](https://ascls.org/the-importance-of-accurate-laboratory-reference-intervals-for-transgender-patients/) are now their own clinical problem: in [one analysis of patients taking testosterone](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214623724000218), 22.4% of creatinine and 40.8% of hemoglobin measurements fell inside one sex's reference range and outside the other's.

**The first responder's problem.** Emergency protocols decide whether to test for pregnancy from reproductive potential, anatomy, and the imaging or drugs about to be ordered — [not from the sex field alone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11187815/), even though the field shapes the reflex. Now put that together with what changed in the last decade. A trans man — documented as male — who still has the organs can be pregnant, and if the male marker is allowed to close the pregnancy question, the workup can go down the wrong branch. And hormone therapy changes the differential in ways the marker doesn't record: [estrogen therapy raises arterial and venous thrombosis risk](https://ashpublications.org/hematology/article/2024/1/652/526228/Gender-affirming-hormone-therapy-in-the), while testosterone therapy causes erythrocytosis — [in one cohort of 282 patients on gender-affirming testosterone](https://www.annfammed.org/content/annalsfm/21/5/403.full.pdf) the cumulative incidence of hematocrit above 50% reached 12.6% within the first 20 months, and [clinical guidance](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5690890/) treats hematocrit above 54% as a reason to stop therapy. "Chest pain and shortness of breath" means something different in a patient on estrogen than it does in the population the algorithm was tuned on. And the tissue doesn't follow the paperwork: [many trans women retain prostate tissue](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2021/03/health-care-for-transgender-and-gender-diverse-individuals), and trans men typically retain breast tissue or residual tissue after chest surgery. Screening has to follow the anatomy that is actually present, not the letter on the form.

**And the other direction, which is where the argument gets its teeth.** Zolpidem — Ambien — was prescribed at the same dose for everyone until [the FDA lowered the recommended starting dose to 5 mg for women in 2013](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/questions-and-answers-risk-next-morning-impairment-after-use-insomnia-drugs-fda-requires-lower), because women clear it more slowly and were waking up still impaired, some of them behind the wheel. Drug-induced QT prolongation and the fatal arrhythmia it causes, torsades de pointes, [carry female sex as an independent risk factor](https://academic.oup.com/toxsci/article/167/2/360/5106018). And women are over-represented in adverse drug reactions generally — [a large part of it traceable to decades of trials that excluded them](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7275616/) or assumed male physiology applied unchanged. That's the same crime in the opposite direction: a system that treated "male" as the default and women as a deviation from it.

Read that list again with the meme in mind. In every case, **the failure came from letting one label stand in for the body — sometimes by treating one sex's default as universal, sometimes by letting the marker contradict the person in front of you.** The label isn't the villain; sex is real, and medicine needs it as data. What fails is the *shortcut*: assuming one category can carry the whole person. That is the argument of this post, arriving from the least arguable direction available.

## The devil's best idea

One more piece, because it completes the loop.

In 1965, the radio commentator Paul Harvey broadcast a piece called *[If I Were the Devil](https://www.wordandwork.org/2018/02/paul-harveys-if-i-were-the-devil-transcript-from-1965/)*. His devil doesn't bomb anything. He works through **convenience, distraction, moral confusion, and the erosion of family and community bonds** — and then, the closing line, he'd simply "keep right on doing what he's doing."

Sixty years later, someone asked a chatbot the same question — *if you were the devil, how would you destroy the minds of the next generation without them knowing?* — and [the answer went viral](https://www.dailydot.com/chatgpt-if-you-were-the-devil), re-shared and re-paraphrased across thousands of threads. The list converges across the versions people collected — endless dopamine, truth redefined as feeling, family structure undermined, everyone taught to blame someone else — and so does one line that appears, in one wording or another, in [the write-ups that kept the full answer](https://medium.com/@alex.trca/chatgpt-prompt-if-you-were-the-devil-what-would-you-do-c90eb3ca7d42):

> Your group is right. The other group is stupid or evil.

Here is my reading of both, and I'll flag it as a *reading* rather than a finding — neither text uses these words. The playbook I see in both is **atomization**: first make people into categories, then make the categories into enemies. Nothing dissolves a community faster than teaching its members to see each other as representatives of something rather than as the people they actually know.

Which is why, in that metaphorical sense, a culture that treats the shallow label as the deepest truth about a person isn't just boring its movies to death — it's helping convert neighbors into factions. Whether that counts as the devil's work depends on your metaphysics. The mechanism doesn't need a devil to run: Goku never had to be Estonian for an Estonian kid to see himself in him, and that is not a bug in human identification. It's the feature that keeps us able to see each other at all.

## What to do with this

I don't think the answer is "stop casting people." The answer is a demotion: **treat the label as a surface fact instead of an identity.** Surface facts are real — sex is real, race is real, history is real, and in a hospital the surface is often the difference between a correct diagnosis and a dead patient. That's exactly why it has to be handled as *data about a body*, not as the *essence of a person.*

The test is the same in every domain. Ask what the thing actually is, underneath:

- In a story: does the character want something, lose something, and change? Or are they a category with dialogue?
- In an identity argument: is this person telling me one new fact about the person I already know — or asking me to accept that the person I knew was never real? The first deserves a welcome, and usually an apology for the years they spent hiding. The second deserves a question — asked of the framework making the demand, not of the person carrying it.
- In a hospital: does the marker match the body in front of me, and does the algorithm have a branch for when it doesn't?

Depth is the defense. Not because depth is virtuous, but because depth is the only layer where two people can actually meet — and it's the layer that every machine built on labels, from a casting brief to a troponin threshold, keeps getting wrong.

The meme was right. Those men were literally Goku. Nobody had to cast him for them.
